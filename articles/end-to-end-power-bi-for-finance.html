My Approach to Building End-to-End Power BI Solutions for Finance

A good finance dashboard starts long before the first chart: with the data, the model and the numbers people need to trust.

By Jamil Ur Rehman · 5 min read

When people see a Power BI dashboard, they see the visuals. What they don't see is the work underneath: understanding the data, checking the numbers, and working out what the business actually needs to know. That's where most of the value is, and where most dashboards go wrong.

As a finance professional, my approach has always been simple:

Keep it simple, focus on what matters, and let the data tell the story.

Here's how that works, step by step.

1. Start with the data, not the dashboard

Before building anything, I explore the data (exploratory data analysis, or EDA) to understand its structure, quality and consistency. I look for missing values, duplicates, inconsistent mappings, figures that don't look right, and anything that won't reconcile.

The aim is to find problems early, while they're cheap to fix, and to make sure the data follows the business and financial logic behind it. A dashboard built on numbers nobody has checked is just a faster way to spread mistakes.

2. Build a pipeline that refreshes itself

Once the data checks out, I decide the best way to bring it into Power BI. Depending on the source and the setup, that could be Excel or database files, APIs, Power BI dataflows (Gen1 or Gen2), shared semantic models or Microsoft Fabric warehouses.

I use Power Query for the cleaning and transformation: setting the right data types, mapping accounts and entities, handling gaps and removing columns nobody needs. The rule I follow is that every step should be repeatable. If a refresh needs someone to fix the data by hand first, the job isn't finished.

3. Get the data model right

The data model is the foundation everything else stands on. I generally use a star schema: one or more fact tables (the transactions) surrounded by clear dimension tables (dates, accounts, entities, customers). It keeps relationships easy to follow, calculations consistent and reports fast.

A snowflake schema can make sense when the underlying data really calls for it. Either way, the priority is a model that is accurate, easy to maintain and fit for the reporting it needs to support. You can see this in my consolidated financial statements project, where one model serves both single-entity and group reporting.

4. Write the financial logic before the visuals

Before I think about charts, I build the DAX measures that explain performance. For finance reporting that usually means:

  • Current-year actuals, budget and prior-year actuals
  • Variances in value and in percentage terms
  • The financial KPIs that matter for the business, such as margins and EBITDA

Then I test those measures across periods, entities and the other key dimensions, to make sure they still give the right answer however the report is sliced. Getting the financial logic right matters far more than making the dashboard look impressive.

5. Design for decisions, not decoration

My design rule is less is more. I put what management needs to see first at the top: clear KPI cards, trends, comparisons and variances, instead of filling every corner of the page with charts.

In finance, the comparison of this year's actuals against budget and last year usually tells you where the business stands and what needs attention. Features like drill-through let users dig into the detail behind a number, and bookmarks help present selected views or highlight a key insight. Every visual has to earn its place by answering a real business question. My FP&A & Financial Performance Dashboard is built on this idea.

6. Review, publish and secure

Before anything goes live, I ask a colleague to review it: to challenge the calculations, check the outputs and spot anything I've missed. A second pair of eyes catches what the developer has stopped seeing.

Then I publish the report to the right Power BI workspace, set up and test row-level security (RLS) where people should only see their own entity or area, and update the Power BI app so the intended audience can access it.

Finally, I check the refresh schedule, the permissions and the experience from the user's side. A report isn't finished just because it works on my desktop. It's finished when it works reliably for the people who depend on it.

Final thoughts

My approach combines financial discipline with data modelling, automation and thoughtful design. Every stage, from checking the raw data to deploying a secure report, adds to how reliable the final result is.

The goal isn't to build the most complicated dashboard. It's to build one that people trust, understand and actually use to make better decisions.

Validate the data. Get the model right. Trust the measures. Keep the design simple. Secure the delivery.
